i've built my career across three countries: nigeria, belgium and the united states. people usually ask which one made me. the honest answer is that each one fixed something the last one couldn't.

## nigeria: build with nothing

i didn't grow up knowing programming was a thing you could do. i was interested in inventing things long before i knew what code was. in 2017 i took a basic java class, and that was it. i've been building ever since.

building in nigeria teaches you a specific kind of discipline, because nothing is guaranteed. power goes. data is expensive. the tools everyone abroad takes for granted cost real money. you learn to make things work with whatever you have, and you learn it fast.

that's where afrowatch came from. data was so expensive that streaming on a phone felt like burning money, so i built a streaming app for people paying for every megabyte. it squeezed more watch time out of each bundle of data, kept watching free and made its money from creators. i was a teenager, and i was building for the exact problem i lived with.

it's also where code space started. in 2021 it was a whatsapp group of young people who wanted to build. today it's a nonprofit of more than 5,000 builders across africa, running workshops, hackathons and events with partners like aws, paystack and wix. nobody gave us a playbook. we just kept showing up.

nigeria gave me speed and resourcefulness. what it didn't give me was structure.

## belgium: build properly

belgium was a different world. things worked, and they worked because people took process seriously.

i studied computer science at hogent in ghent and worked as a software engineer on systems that schools relied on every day. that work taught me something nigeria never had to: when real people depend on what you ship, "it works on my machine" isn't good enough. you plan, you test, you document, and you keep it running long after the excitement of launching wears off.

i also interned at microsoft in brussels, working on azure deployment automation. it was my first look at engineering at real scale, where the hard part isn't writing the code but making sure it never breaks for anyone.

belgium gave me craft. it slowed me down in the best way. but it also showed me how easy it is to get comfortable.

## the united states: build big

the us is where ambition is normal. nobody here thinks it's strange to try something huge.

i interned at apple as an ml engineer, working on intent classification for siri. then i came to morgan state to study computer science, and started researching autonomous drones for disaster response, perception and planning that let a uav spot fire and flooding and route around it on its own. that work turned into two papers at itc 2026.

i co-founded fathom, voice-first code intelligence for engineering teams, and we took 2nd at openai's startup hackathon. now i'm building lane, putting an agent in the middle of how independent car dealers run their business.

the us gave me scale. it taught me to stop asking whether something is too big to try.

## why i needed all three

if i had only had nigeria, i'd be fast but messy. only belgium, careful but too cautious. only the united states, ambitious but maybe out of touch with what it means to build without resources.

together they gave me a way of working i still use every day: build like nothing is guaranteed, finish it like people depend on it, and aim like the ceiling isn't there.

that's also why i still build for places like where i started. the best products i've worked on came from a problem i'd lived with myself, and three countries gave me a lot of problems to live with.
