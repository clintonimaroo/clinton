For most of my first year as a programmer, I did not own a laptop.

I was a teenager in Abuja, and I had just finished a Java bootcamp. Since I had nothing to run my code on, I wrote it by hand in a notebook. Whenever I could, I walked to a friend's house near our church, because his area had steady electricity and ours did not. There, I typed everything in to see whether any of it worked. Most of the time it did not. I would fix what I could on his laptop, go home, and start writing the next thing on paper.

That was 2017. Since then I have built a career across three countries: Nigeria, Belgium and the United States. I have worked at Apple and Microsoft, started a community that now has more than 5,000 members, and co-founded a startup that did not make it.

People often ask me which country was the best one to build in. I think that is the wrong question, because each of them taught me something I could not have learned in the other two. This is my attempt to write those lessons down, in case they are useful to someone who is starting where I started.

## Nigeria taught me to start before I was ready

I did not grow up around technology. As a child in Benin City, I was good at practical things. I kept a small poultry farm while I was still in primary school, and I sold my drawings to older students, which is how I paid for lunch. When I later moved to Abuja to live with my uncle, I spent my free time making sandals out of leftover material from the local shoemaker.

It was my uncle who introduced me to programming. One day he asked me whether I knew that someone had written code to make every button on my phone work. I did not. I had never even heard the word "coding". He signed me up for a bootcamp during the long holiday, and I finished the six-month programme in under two months. The first real thing I built was a calculator for Windows, followed by a tic-tac-toe game, which we call X and O.

What I remember most about that period is how little I had. I had no laptop, the power went out all the time, and I knew exactly one other person who was learning to code. If I had waited for the right conditions, I would never have started. So I worked with what was in front of me, and for a while that was a notebook and a pen. My mum supported me from the start, and I am still grateful for that.

The same instinct led to my first product. Whenever there was a big football match or a new season of Big Brother Naija, my friends and I had the same problem. Either there was no electricity, or the TV subscription had expired. I wanted people to be able to watch on their phones without using too much data, so my friend Precious and I built a streaming app and called it Afrowatch. I had only ever written desktop software in Java, which meant I had to learn HTML, CSS and JavaScript while building it. I learned more from that one project than from any course I have taken since.

Code Space began in a similar way. It was a WhatsApp group where two friends and I, all teenagers, shared resources and talked about the things we wanted to build one day. Then a magazine asked to interview me. The person who ran the interview was Isabella, and at one point she asked about the group. I told her I wanted it to be a place for young people who felt they were not doing enough and had nobody to learn with, because that was exactly how I had felt. Some time later she got back in touch and asked to join the team. She is now my co-founder. We opened a Telegram group, more than 500 people joined, and we registered Code Space as a nonprofit. Today it has over 5,000 members across Africa.

None of those things started with a plan. They started with a problem I was living with and whatever tools I had at the time. That is the first lesson I would pass on: you do not need permission or perfect conditions to begin. You need a problem you care about and the willingness to look a little foolish while you work on it.

## Belgium taught me that going backwards can be progress

I moved to Belgium to finish secondary school, and I arrived expecting to carry on from where I had stopped. Instead, the school told me that I could not join a regular class until I spoke Dutch. I spent more than a year in a language school before I was allowed to continue my education.

After that I made things harder for myself by choosing the wrong course. I enrolled in electrical engineering, because I thought it was close to the hands-on work I had always enjoyed. It was not, and I was learning it in a language I had only just picked up. A year later I switched to programming. Between the language school and that detour, I ended up two years behind where I had expected to be.

For a long time I thought of those years as lost. I see them differently now, because of what happened next.

The organisation that ran my language school was called GO! Talent, and I did an internship in its IT department. It was not glamorous work. We were the people everyone called when something stopped working, whether that was a single computer or the lights in an entire building. But I did the job well, and when the internship ended my manager recommended me for a role as a software engineer. For the first time, I was building systems that schools depended on every day. When something broke, it affected every school that relied on us. Nigeria had taught me how to get something working. Belgium taught me how to keep it working.

That experience led to an internship at Microsoft in Brussels, where I learned how carefully large companies ship software. Later I joined Apple as a machine learning intern on a team based in San Francisco, while I was still living in Belgium. I still remember the day it sank in: the boy who used to write Java in a notebook was now working on Siri.

So the second lesson is about patience. When you move to a new country, you will almost certainly have to take a few steps back. You may need to learn a language, repeat a year or accept a job that is below your ability. It feels like failure while it is happening. In my case, the job I was overqualified for was the one that opened every door that came after it.

## The United States taught me that timing matters as much as the idea

I eventually left Belgium because studying computer science in Dutch was becoming a struggle, and I had learned that my diploma would be difficult to use in other countries. I transferred to a university in the United States with close to 70 credits. The university accepted 20 of them, which set me back once again.

In my first year there, two friends and I entered the OpenAI startup hackathon in New York. We nearly did not go, and we only booked our tickets at the last minute. When we walked in, there were more than 200 teams in the room. Many of them were established startups with paying customers, and the judges were investors. I turned to one of my teammates and told him this was going to be difficult.

We had one advantage, which was the idea. I wanted to build a tool that let engineers ask questions about a codebase by voice and hear the answers spoken back. Voice AI had not taken off yet, but I was convinced it soon would. We built a working version during the event, made it into the final six, and gave a live demo on the main stage. We finished second.

That project became Fathom. Within a few months 258 engineers were using it in a closed beta, and they were sending more than 60,000 queries a month. We also had something even more valuable, which was a head start in a market that almost nobody else had entered.

We did not use that head start. My co-founders and I were at different universities, and both of them were in their final semester. We worked on Fathom remotely, in between classes and exams, when we should have been in one room giving it everything we had. By the time we were all free to do that, voice AI was everywhere and our head start was gone.

I have thought about that a great deal. The idea was good and the early numbers were strong, but neither of those things was enough. The third lesson is the one that cost me the most to learn: a good idea has a window, and you have to move while it is open.

## What I would tell my sixteen-year-old self

If I could go back to that boy in Abuja with his notebook, I would not tell him that it gets easier, because it does not. I would tell him three things.

The first is to start now, with whatever he has. The second is not to panic when life sets him back, because the setbacks are often where the real learning happens. The third is that when something starts to work, he should give it everything before the moment passes.

I am still learning the third one. Today I am building Lane, an AI agent that runs the back office for car dealerships, and I am researching how drones can find their own way through disaster zones. I do not know yet which of these will work. What I do know is that I will not wait for perfect conditions, and I will not waste a head start again.

I no longer need a notebook to write code. But I try to build with the same determination I had when a notebook was all I had.
